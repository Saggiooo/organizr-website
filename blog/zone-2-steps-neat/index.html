Zone 2, steps and NEAT: the boring cardio that works

Most of the cardio that changes your body is slow, dull and uncounted. What zone 2 actually feels like, and why a step target beats another HIIT class.

The cardio that gets the attention is the kind that leaves you on the floor: intervals, circuits, classes with a countdown timer and loud music. The cardio that quietly does most of the work is the opposite. Long, easy sessions at a pace you could hold a conversation at. Walking to the shop instead of driving. Taking the stairs. None of it looks impressive, and most of it is not even counted.

This article is about that boring layer: what zone 2 is, why a daily step target is so useful, and what NEAT is and why it matters more than most workouts.

Zone 2, without the jargon

Training zones split effort into bands, usually five, from very easy to all-out. Zone 2 is the second band: easy, but not nothing. You are clearly exercising, breathing a little harder, but you could keep going for a long time.

The simplest way to find it is the talk test. In zone 2 you can speak in full sentences, but you would not want to sing. If you can only get out a few words at a time, you are above it. If you could recite a poem without effort, you are probably below it.

Heart rate monitors and watches can help, but zone formulas based on age are rough estimates and vary a lot between people. The talk test is free and surprisingly reliable.

Why easy cardio is worth doing

  • You can do a lot of it. Easy sessions cost little recovery, so they fit next to strength training without stealing from it. See cardio and weights in the same week.
  • It builds an aerobic base. Endurance athletes spend most of their training time at easy intensities, and a better base makes hard sessions and long days more manageable.
  • It is repeatable. Nobody dreads a 40-minute easy bike ride the way they dread intervals. What you do not dread, you keep doing.
  • It is easy to schedule. A walk, a ride to work, a podcast on the treadmill. It fits around life instead of demanding a slot of its own.

The common mistake is not doing too little zone 2. It is doing every cardio session at a medium-hard effort that is too hard to recover from easily and too easy to be real interval work. If most of your cardio feels “somewhat hard”, slowing down is often the upgrade.

Steps: the metric that counts what workouts miss

The famous 10,000 steps target did not come out of a lab. It is generally traced back to a Japanese pedometer launched in the 1960s, whose name translates roughly as “10,000-step meter”. A round number sold well.

That does not make step counting useless. It makes the specific number less sacred. The real value of a step target is that it measures movement you would otherwise never record: the walk to the station, the trip up the stairs, pacing during a phone call. Those add up to far more time than your workouts.

A practical approach:

  1. Look at your average daily steps over the last two weeks. That is your baseline.
  2. Set a target a little above it, for example 1,500–2,000 steps higher.
  3. Hold it for a month, then raise it again if it felt easy.

Someone averaging 4,000 steps who reaches 7,000 has made a bigger change than someone going from 10,000 to 12,000, even though the second number sounds more impressive.

NEAT: the part of your day nobody talks about

NEAT stands for non-exercise activity thermogenesis: the energy you use moving around when you are not exercising. Walking, standing, cooking, cleaning, fidgeting, carrying the shopping.

It varies enormously between people. In a well-known 1999 study published in Science, James Levine and colleagues overfed volunteers by the same amount for eight weeks. Some gained far more fat than others, and the biggest predictor of the difference was how much their everyday non-exercise movement increased. Some people instinctively moved more when eating more, others did not.

The practical point is simple. An hour at the gym is one hour out of sixteen or so awake hours. What you do in the other fifteen matters just as much, and it often drops without you noticing: when you are tired, when you are dieting, when work moves you from the office to the sofa.

How to raise it without trying very hard

  • Attach walking to things you already do. Calls on foot, a walk after lunch, getting off one stop early. See habit stacking.
  • Make stairs the default. Decide once, stop deciding every time.
  • Break up long sitting. Stand up every hour, refill the water bottle, walk to talk to a colleague.
  • Pick errands that involve walking. The farther parking spot, the shop around the corner.

The short version

  • Zone 2 is easy cardio you can talk through. Use the talk test.
  • Most of your cardio should be easy; slowing down is often the fix.
  • Step targets are useful because they count movement workouts miss. Raise yours gradually from your own baseline.
  • NEAT, the movement outside the gym, varies hugely and deserves attention.
