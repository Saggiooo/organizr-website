Progressive overload, explained without a spreadsheet

Adding weight is only one of five ways to overload a lift. How to progress reps, sets, tempo and range of motion, and how to know when you actually did.

On this page

    Progressive overload is the one principle every training program agrees on: to keep getting stronger, you have to keep asking your body for a little more than last time. The problem is that most people hear “a little more” and think only of the number on the bar. Add 2.5 kg every week and in a year you will be benching a small car. That is not how it goes, and when the plates stop moving, people conclude the program is broken.

    It is not broken. Load is only one of the levers. Here are the others, how to pull them in the right order, and how to tell whether you actually progressed or just had a good day.

    The five levers of overload

    Anything that makes the same exercise a bit harder than last time counts as overload. In practice there are five ways to do it:

    1. Load. More weight for the same reps. The obvious one, and the one that runs out first.
    2. Reps. Same weight, one or two more reps. On most accessory lifts this is where the progress really lives.
    3. Sets. One more working set for the same exercise. Useful in small doses, because volume piles up fast.
    4. Range of motion. A deeper squat, a full stretch at the bottom of a row, a pause on the chest. Same load, more work.
    5. Tempo and control. A slower lowering phase or a pause at the hardest point. Harder to measure, so use it deliberately and write it down.

    A sixth lever gets less attention: effort at the same numbers. If last month 80 kg for 8 felt like you had one rep left and today it feels like you had three, you got stronger even though the log looks identical. That is why effort is worth recording, more on that below.

    Double progression: the method that works for almost everyone

    If you only take one idea from this article, take this one. Pick a rep range instead of a fixed number, for example 6-10 reps. Then:

    1. Start with a weight you can lift for 6 clean reps on every set.
    2. Each session, try to add a rep to one or more sets while keeping the form the same.
    3. When you hit 10 reps on every set, add the smallest increment available and drop back to around 6.

    That is it. Double progression works because it gives you somewhere to go on the weeks when adding weight is not realistic. You are never stuck: either the reps are going up, or the weight is. It also stops the classic mistake of jumping 5 kg on an exercise where 5 kg is a huge percentage of the working weight, like lateral raises or curls.

    Small lifts need small jumps. Going from 8 kg to 10 kg dumbbells is a 25% increase. Going from 100 kg to 102.5 kg on a squat is 2.5%. Treat them differently.

    How fast should the numbers move?

    Much slower than the internet suggests, and the rate depends on how long you have been training.

    • First months. Beginners can often add load to big lifts session to session. Enjoy it, it does not last.
    • After the first year or so. Progress shifts from session to session to week to week, then month to month. Adding a rep a week on a given exercise is a very good rate.
    • Several years in. Progress is measured over training blocks of weeks. A few kilos on a main lift in a quarter is real progress.

    The practical consequence: if you are past the beginner stage and your log shows the same numbers for three or four weeks, that is not a failure, it is information. Change one variable, not five.

    Effort: the lever that makes the others honest

    Overload only means something if the effort is comparable. Ten reps with two left in the tank and ten reps grinding to failure are not the same set. The simplest way to keep that honest is reps in reserve (RIR): after a set, estimate how many more reps you could have done with good form. Researchers and coaches use the same scale, and it maps onto the familiar RPE numbers (RIR 2 is roughly RPE 8).

    Most working sets land well around 1-3 RIR. You do not need to fail to progress, and failing every set on heavy compound lifts mostly makes the next session worse. Record the RIR next to the reps and you can compare sessions properly: 80 kg × 8 at RIR 1 last month and 80 kg × 8 at RIR 3 today means you are stronger, even though the weight did not move.

    When progress stalls

    A plateau on one exercise for a couple of weeks is normal. A plateau on everything for a month is a signal. Before changing the program, check the boring things in this order:

    1. Consistency. Did you actually train on the days you planned? Missed sessions look like plateaus from the inside.
    2. Sleep and stress. A rough month at work shows up on the bar. See why sleep is a training variable.
    3. Food. Progressing hard while eating in a big deficit is swimming upstream. That is not a moral failing, just a trade-off to be aware of.
    4. Accumulated fatigue. If everything feels heavy, a lighter week often unlocks more than a new program. See deload weeks.
    5. The exercise itself. Only then consider swapping a variation or changing the rep range.

    What to log so you can see overload

    You cannot overload what you do not remember. Most people think they remember their last session, and most people are wrong by a rep or two, which is exactly the size of the progress you are looking for. The minimum useful log per exercise is weight, reps per set and effort (RIR). Add a short note when something changes: new grip, new machine, a pause, a bad night.

    The real payoff comes from comparing against the previous session of the same workout, set by set.     The short version

    • Load is one lever out of five. Reps, sets, range of motion and tempo count too.
    • Use a rep range and double progression: reps first, then weight.
    • Record effort, so that “same numbers, easier” counts as progress.
    • Expect progress to slow as you get more experienced. Measure in weeks, then months.
    • Compare every session to the previous one. That comparison is the whole game.
