Ninety-day goals beat New Year resolutions

A year is too long to stay accountable and a week is too short to change anything. Why a quarter is the unit that fits both training and life.

New Year resolutions have a bad reputation, and it is only partly deserved. Setting a goal on 1 January is not the problem. The problem is the size of the unit. A year is too long to stay accountable to: in February the deadline feels infinitely far away, and by October it is too late to fix anything. A week, on the other hand, is too short to change anything that matters.

A quarter, roughly ninety days, sits in the sweet spot. Here is why, and how to use it.

Resolutions work better than their reputation

It is worth being fair to resolutions first. In a study by John Norcross and colleagues published in 2002, people who made New Year resolutions were far more likely to have achieved their goal six months later than people with similar goals who did not formally resolve anything: about 46% against about 4%. Deciding to change, at a clear moment, helps.

Researchers call this the fresh start effect. Work by Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis found that people are more likely to start pursuing goals after temporal landmarks: the start of a year, a month, a week, a birthday. The new period feels like a clean slate.

So the landmark helps. What goes wrong is everything after it.

Why a year is the wrong unit

  • No urgency. A goal due in December can always start next month. So it does.
  • Plans go stale. Your life in March is not the life you planned for in January. A job changes, a child arrives, an injury happens, and the plan never gets updated.
  • Feedback is too slow. If you only check a goal once a year, you find out it failed when it is too late to adjust.
  • One landmark, one chance. Miss the January start and the next “fresh start” feels a year away.

Why ninety days works

  • Long enough to change something real. Twelve or thirteen weeks is enough for a visible strength gain, a new habit to become automatic for many people, or a meaningful saving target.
  • Short enough to feel urgent. Week three of twelve is already a quarter of the way through. You feel the clock.
  • Four fresh starts a year. If a quarter goes badly, the next landmark is weeks away, not months.
  • A natural review rhythm. At the end of each quarter you look back, keep what worked and change what did not.
A year of goals is four quarters, not one long hope.

How to set a good ninety-day goal

Pick one to three goals, not ten

Every goal competes for the same time and attention. One main goal and one or two smaller ones is plenty for a quarter.

Make it an approach goal

A 2020 study by Martin Oscarsson and colleagues followed over a thousand people with New Year resolutions for a year. People with approach goals (“start running twice a week”) were more successful than those with avoidance goals (“stop eating sweets”): around 59% against 47%. Frame the goal as something to do, not something to stop.

Separate outcome and process

The outcome is what you want to be true in ninety days: “deadlift 120 kg”, “save 1,500 €”, “read six books”. The process is what you will do each week to get there: “train three times”, “move 125 € on payday”, “read 20 minutes a night”. You control the process, not the outcome, so the process is what you track day to day.

Write the weekly habits down

Each goal should break down into one or two habits you can check off. Those habits are the goal in daily form. See habit stacking for how to anchor them.

The quarterly review

At the end of each quarter, spend half an hour answering four questions:

  1. Did I hit the outcome? If not, how close did I get?
  2. How consistent was the process? What was my completion rate on the habits?
  3. What got in the way, and was it predictable?
  4. For the next quarter: keep the goal, raise it, lower it, or replace it?

A goal you missed with 85% consistency on the habits is usually a goal that was slightly too ambitious, not a failure. A goal you missed with 30% consistency is telling you something about the plan, the timing, or how much you actually want it.

Still want a yearly goal? Nest it

Some goals really are yearly: a marathon in October, a big trip, a savings target. Keep them, but split them into four quarterly stages with their own milestones. The yearly goal gives direction; the quarter gives urgency.

The short version

  • Fresh starts help, but a year is too long to stay accountable.
  • Ninety days is long enough to change something and short enough to feel urgent.
  • Pick one to three approach goals, each broken into weekly habits.
  • Review at the end of every quarter and adjust.
  • Nest yearly goals into quarterly stages.
