What a year of training actually costs

Membership, supplements, kit, the coach you used for two months and the subscriptions you forgot. A full year priced out, and where the waste usually is.

Ask someone how much their training costs and they will usually name one number: the gym membership. Ask them to add up everything they actually spent last year and the number tends to double. Shoes, kit, supplements, the coach they tried for two months, the app subscriptions, the class pack that expired half-used, the protein bars at the petrol station.

None of that is necessarily wasted. But you cannot decide what is worth it until you see the whole bill. Here is a full year priced out, and where the waste usually hides.

An example year

The numbers below are an illustrative example for someone training three or four times a week at a mid-range gym in a European city. Prices vary a lot by country and city, so the point is the structure, not the exact figures. Replace them with your own.

  • Gym membership: around 40 € a month, so about 480 € a year. Often the single biggest line.
  • Sign-up or annual fees: 30–60 €. Easy to forget because it is paid once.
  • Training shoes: one or two pairs, 80–200 €. Runners often need more.
  • Clothing: a few shirts, shorts, a hoodie. 80–150 €.
  • Small kit: a lifting belt, straps, bands, a bottle, a lock, a bag. 50–120 €, much of it bought once.
  • Supplements and sports food: protein powder, bars, drinks. Anywhere from zero to 50 € a month, so 0–600 € a year.
  • Coaching or classes: a few personal training sessions or a class pack. 0–500 € or more.
  • Apps and subscriptions: a training app, a nutrition app, a music service you mostly use at the gym. 0–150 €.
  • Getting there: fuel, parking or transport. Easy to ignore, and for a gym 20 minutes away by car it can add up to a few hundred euros a year.

Add those up and the total for a fairly normal year lands somewhere between about 800 and 2,000 €, depending mostly on supplements, coaching and transport. That is two to four times the membership alone.

Cost per session: the number that actually matters

A more useful way to look at it is to divide the total by the number of sessions you actually did. That depends on how consistent you were, not on what you planned.

  • 1,200 € a year and 150 sessions is 8 € a session. Reasonable.
  • The same 1,200 € and 50 sessions is 24 € a session. The same spending, three times the price.

This is why consistency is also a financial question. The cheapest way to make training cheaper is usually to train more often with what you already pay for. See how to build a gym habit that survives week three.

The most expensive gym membership is the one you pay for and do not use.

Where the waste usually is

Unused memberships

The classic. January sign-up, regular until March, then paying for months while going rarely. If your attendance has been low for two or three months, look at a cheaper off-peak membership, a pay-per-visit option, or a pause, if your gym offers one.

Forgotten subscriptions

Training apps, streaming workouts, a nutrition premium tier you tried once. Each is small, which is exactly why they survive. Many auto-renew yearly, so the charge arrives when you have forgotten it exists.

Supplements beyond the basics

For most people training for general fitness, the supplement aisle is optional. Protein powder can be a convenient way to eat more protein, but it is food, not magic. The more expensive and exotic the product, the more carefully it is worth asking whether it is doing anything you can see in your log.

Kit bought for motivation

New gear feels like progress. It is not. A good pair of shoes you use weekly is money well spent; the third set of resistance bands is not.

Coaching you did not use fully

A coach can be one of the best investments in training, particularly at the start or when learning complex lifts. The waste is paying for sessions or a monthly plan you do not follow. If you hire someone, schedule the sessions in advance, like any other appointment.

How to see your own numbers

  1. Go through the last twelve months of bank statements and card payments.
  2. Tag every training-related expense, including transport and food bought only because of training.
  3. Group them into the categories above.
  4. Count your sessions for the same year from your training log.
  5. Divide. Then decide which lines are worth keeping.

The short version

  • The membership is often less than half of what a year of training really costs.
  • Price it per session, not per year. Consistency lowers the cost of everything.
  • Waste hides in unused memberships, forgotten subscriptions, supplements and kit.
  • Review your statements once, then track training spending as it happens.
