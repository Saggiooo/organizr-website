Push pull legs or full body: which split fits your week

The best split is the one you can repeat on a bad week. How to choose between full body, upper lower and push pull legs based on days, not on theory.

Few gym arguments last as long as the split debate. Push pull legs, full body, upper lower, bro split: each has fans who swear by it and a stack of anecdotes to prove it. The honest answer is less exciting. For most people, the split matters much less than two other things: how many days a week you can reliably train, and how much quality work each muscle gets across that week.

So instead of asking which split is best, ask which split survives your real calendar. Here is how to answer that.

What the research actually says

Studies comparing splits keep landing on the same conclusion. Training a muscle about twice a week tends to beat once a week when total work is not matched, mostly because it is easier to get more good sets in. When weekly volume is equal, the difference between frequencies shrinks to very little. In plain words: weekly hard sets per muscle and consistency drive results; the split is the container you put them in.

That shifts the question. A split is good if it lets you accumulate enough quality sets without turning each session into a two-hour marathon, and if it bends instead of breaking when life gets in the way.

The three main options

Full body

Every session trains the whole body, usually with one or two exercises per major pattern: a squat or leg press, a hinge, a push, a pull, maybe an accessory or two.

  • Best for: 2–3 days a week, beginners, anyone whose schedule is unpredictable.
  • Strength: missing a session costs little, because every muscle is hit again in a couple of days.
  • Weakness: sessions get long if you try to add too much, and heavy squats and deadlifts in the same workout wear you out.

Upper lower

Alternate upper body days and lower body days, typically four sessions a week: upper, lower, rest, upper, lower.

  • Best for: 4 days a week, intermediate lifters.
  • Strength: every muscle twice a week, with sessions of reasonable length.
  • Weakness: lower days are demanding, and upper days can get crowded with pushing and pulling work.

Push pull legs

Push day (chest, shoulders, triceps), pull day (back, biceps), leg day. Run once through for three sessions a week, or twice through for six.

  • Best for: 5–6 days a week, people who enjoy training often and have the time.
  • Strength: sessions are focused, easy to structure, and allow a lot of volume per muscle.
  • Weakness: at three days a week each muscle gets trained only once. Miss a leg day and the legs wait a full week.

Choose by days, not by theory

The quickest way to decide is to look back at the last eight weeks, not forward at your ideal week. How many days did you actually train, on average? Pick the split that fits that number, then add a day only once you have hit it consistently for a month.

  • 2 days: full body, both days. Nothing else gets each muscle trained twice.
  • 3 days: full body is the default. PPL once through works if you accept once-a-week frequency.
  • 4 days: upper lower. Or full body four times with lighter sessions.
  • 5 days: upper lower plus a full body day, or PPL plus upper and lower.
  • 6 days: PPL twice through, if you recover well and enjoy it.
The best split is the one you can repeat on a bad week. A four-day plan done three days a week becomes a program with holes in it.

The missed-session test

Before committing, run a simple thought experiment: what happens to this split when you miss one session? With full body, nothing much: the next session covers everything. With upper lower, one half of the body waits a few extra days. With PPL at three days, a whole muscle group can go ten days without work.

If your weeks often include travel, late meetings or kids getting sick, pick the split that fails gracefully. You can always move to a more specialised one when your schedule settles.

How to tell whether your split is working

A split works if three things are true over a couple of months:

  1. You complete most of the planned sessions. Look at the calendar, not your memory.
  2. The main lifts are progressing, even slowly. See progressive overload, explained.
  3. You still want to train. A split that bores you is a split you will drop.

Switching splits without losing progress

Changing split is not starting over. Keep the same main exercises and rep ranges, redistribute them across the new days, and compare the first two weeks against your previous numbers. Expect a small dip while you get used to the new order. After that, if the numbers keep climbing, the switch worked.

And resist changing more than once every couple of months. Most of the benefit of any split comes from running it long enough to see what it does.

The short version

  • Weekly quality sets and consistency matter more than the split itself.
  • Choose the split for the days you actually train, not the ones you plan to.
  • 2–3 days: full body. 4 days: upper lower. 5–6 days: push pull legs.
  • Pick the split that survives a missed session.
