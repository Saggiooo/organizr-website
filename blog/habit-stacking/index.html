Habit stacking: bolt the new habit onto one you already have

New habits fail because they have no trigger. Anchor them to something already automatic — coffee, the commute, closing the laptop — and the trigger comes free.

On this page

    Most new habits do not fail because they are hard. They fail because nothing reminds you to do them. You decide to stretch every day, drink more water, read ten pages, and for a few days you remember. Then a busy morning comes, you forget once, then twice, and the habit is gone. It was never attached to anything.

    Habit stacking fixes exactly that. Instead of hoping to remember, you bolt the new habit onto something you already do automatically, so the old habit becomes the trigger for the new one.

    Where the idea comes from

    The term was popularised by James Clear in Atomic Habits, building on the work of Stanford researcher BJ Fogg, whose Tiny Habits method uses the same structure. Both rest on a large body of research into habits and on a technique psychologists call implementation intentions: plans of the form “when X happens, I will do Y”.

    The reasoning is simple. Habits are behaviours triggered by a cue. You already have dozens of reliable cues every day: waking up, making coffee, brushing your teeth, sitting at your desk, getting home. Each one is a hook you can hang something new on.

    The formula

    After I [current habit], I will [new habit].

    That is all it is. A few examples:

    • After I pour my morning coffee, I will write down today’s priority.
    • After I sit down at my desk, I will fill my water bottle.
    • After I close the laptop at the end of the day, I will put my gym bag by the door.
    • After I brush my teeth at night, I will read two pages.
    • After I get off the train, I will walk the long way home.

    How to pick a good anchor

    The anchor is the most important part. A weak anchor means a weak habit. Good anchors are:

    • Already automatic. Something you do every day without thinking. Making coffee, yes. “Checking my goals”, no.
    • Specific. “After breakfast” is vague, breakfast can be anywhere. “After I put my cup in the dishwasher” is a precise moment.
    • At the right time and place. The new habit should make sense right there. Stretching fits after getting out of the shower. It does not fit after arriving at the office.
    • Roughly the same frequency. A daily habit needs a daily anchor. A three-times-a-week habit can hang on a specific weekday event.

    A useful exercise: write two lists. One of things you do every day without fail. One of things that happen to you every day (the alarm goes off, you arrive home, the kettle boils). Those lists are your anchors.

    Make the new habit tiny

    Fogg’s key insight is that the new habit should start ridiculously small. Not “do 30 minutes of mobility” but “do one stretch”. Not “read a chapter” but “read one page”.

    It sounds like it will not achieve anything, and in the first week it barely does. The point is to build the link between anchor and action. Once the link is automatic, expanding the habit is easy; you usually do it without planning to. Starting big means relying on motivation, and motivation is exactly what fails. See motivation runs out, systems don’t.

    Building chains

    Once one stack is solid, you can extend it. Each new habit becomes the anchor for the next one:

    1. After I switch off the alarm, I will drink a glass of water.
    2. After I drink the water, I will open the curtains.
    3. After I open the curtains, I will write down today’s priority.

    That is how a morning routine gets built without willpower. But add links one at a time, and only when the previous one feels automatic. A chain with a weak link breaks at that link. See the morning routine myth for why short routines beat long ones.

    Common mistakes

    • Choosing an anchor you do not actually do daily. “After my workout” only works on training days.
    • Stacking too much on one anchor. Five new habits after coffee is a routine, not a stack, and it collapses together.
    • Making the new habit too big. If you skip it on a bad day, it is too big.
    • Not writing it down. A stack that lives only in your head is just an intention.

    Tracking it

    For the first few weeks, tracking helps you notice where the chain breaks. If the new habit keeps getting skipped, the anchor is probably wrong, or the habit is too big. Change one of the two.

    The short version

    • New habits fail mostly for lack of a trigger.
    • Use the formula: after I [current habit], I will [new habit].
    • Pick anchors that are automatic, specific and in the right place.
    • Start tiny; grow the habit once the link is automatic.
    • Add links to the chain one at a time.
